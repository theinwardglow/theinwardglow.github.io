I took magnesium glycinate every night for 30 days. Here is what actually happened.

Not the version that sounds like an ad. The honest one, including the nights it didn't seem to do anything at all.

6 min readSupplement reviewAffiliate links disclosed

I want to start with something nobody says in these posts. The first few nights I took magnesium glycinate, I noticed nothing. Zero. I went to bed, I lay there, I fell asleep about the same time I always did. I almost stopped.

I am glad I didn't.

By around night five or six something shifted. I wasn't falling asleep faster necessarily but I was staying asleep. The 3am wake-up that had become my normal, just gone. I would open my eyes and it would be 6am and I genuinely couldn't remember the last time that happened.

Not the cheap kind. Not the wrong kind. Magnesium glycinate specifically. The form matters more than I realized.

Why glycinate specifically and not the regular kind

I had tried regular magnesium before. The cheap oxide kind from the drugstore. It did nothing for my sleep and gave me an upset stomach. I almost wrote off the whole category because of that.

What I didn't know is that different forms of magnesium do completely different things in your body. Magnesium oxide has poor absorption. Magnesium glycinate is the form that actually reaches the brain and nervous system, because glycine, the amino acid it is bound to, is itself a calming neurotransmitter. So you are essentially getting two things working for you at once in one capsule.

I started with 200mg about an hour before bed. After two weeks I bumped it to 300mg and that was my sweet spot. Some women do 400mg. Start low, give it two weeks, and pay attention to how you feel.

What changed and what didn't

What changed: I stopped waking at 3am. My sleep felt deeper, less fragmented. I was less wired when I got into bed. The low-level hum of anxious energy I used to carry into the night started settling about 30 to 45 minutes after taking it.

What didn't change: I still needed everything else. The consistent wake time, the wind-down routine, the phone out of the bedroom. Magnesium isn't a switch you flip. It lowers the floor so the rest can do their job.

What to look for when you buy it

  • The label must say magnesium glycinate or magnesium bisglycinate. Not oxide, not citrate, not "magnesium complex."
  • Third-party tested is worth the extra few dollars. NSF or USP certification means what's on the label is in the capsule.
  • Capsule form absorbs better than tablet for most people.
  • Skip the gummies. The doses are usually too low and the added sugar works against you.

Why I kept waking up at 3am every single night, and the two things that finally stopped it

For months I thought something was wrong with me. Turns out something was, but it wasn't what I expected.

5 min readSleep tips

It was so consistent it started to feel like an alarm. 3am, sometimes 3:15, eyes open, heart going a little fast, mind immediately running. Every night. For months.

I had tried everything I could think of. More melatonin. Less melatonin. Earlier bedtime. Chamomile tea. White noise. None of it touched the 3am wake-up.

What I didn't know then, and what changed everything once I finally got it, is that waking up at the same time every night is almost never a sleep problem. It is a cortisol problem.

What cortisol has to do with it

Cortisol is your stress hormone. It naturally starts climbing in the early morning hours to get your body ready to wake up. But if it's out of rhythm, from stress, blood sugar issues, or just a nervous system that never got the memo to relax, it can spike way too early. Like 3am early instead of 6am early.

When that happens you snap awake. Not gradually. Suddenly. Often with a slight feeling of anxiety even though nothing is wrong. That was me exactly.

The 3am wake-up is not your body failing you. It is your body trying to protect you from something that is not actually happening. Your job is to convince it you are safe and there is nothing to fight right now.

The two things that stopped it for me

The first was magnesium glycinate taken before bed. Magnesium helps regulate cortisol and supports the nervous system overnight. Within about two weeks of taking it consistently my 3am wake-ups started getting less frequent. Within a month they were rare.

The second was ashwagandha. I started it for daytime stress but what I noticed first was the 3am issue. Ashwagandha works directly on cortisol levels over time. It is not an overnight fix, you need to take it consistently for a few weeks to feel the difference, but for the 3am waker it addresses the root cause in a way that melatonin never could.

I also started doing the 4-4-6 breath if I did wake up. In for four, hold for four, out for six. The longer exhale activates your parasympathetic nervous system, your calm response. Five rounds and I was usually back asleep within fifteen minutes.

What to do tonight

  • Do not reach for your phone if you wake. Not even to check the time. The light and stimulation make it worse.
  • Try the 4-4-6 breath lying on your back before you do anything else.
  • Have a small protein snack before bed. A few nuts or a spoon of nut butter can stabilize blood sugar overnight and reduce that early cortisol spike.
  • Consider magnesium glycinate. Give it two genuine weeks before you decide.

The brain dump method: how five minutes and a notebook quieted my racing mind

I tried meditation apps. I tried breathing in bed. Then I tried this. It sounds too simple. It is not.

4 min readCalm mind

My mind does not stop. I have accepted this about myself. The moment I get into bed it starts, what needs to happen tomorrow, the thing I said at work last Tuesday, whether I turned off the stove, the email I forgot to send.

I tried meditation apps. I tried breathing exercises in bed. I tried telling myself to stop thinking, which as you probably know does exactly nothing.

What finally worked was not trying to quiet my mind at all. It was giving my mind somewhere to put everything before I got in bed.

What the brain dump actually is

It is not journaling. It is not gratitude lists. It is not structured or pretty. It is five minutes with a piece of paper before bed where you write down everything that is in your head. Every to-do, every worry, every thing you remembered you need to do, every thought that has been circling.

Then at the bottom you write: "I have written this down. It will be here tomorrow. I don't need to hold it tonight." And you close the notebook.

The reason it works is neurological. Your brain loops because it doesn't trust you to remember things. It keeps replaying them to make sure they don't get lost. When you write them down, your brain's job is done. It can let go.

Your brain is not trying to torture you at night. It is trying to help you. It just needs to know the list is safe somewhere.

How I do it

I keep a notebook on my nightstand. Every night before I get into bed, not in bed, before, I set a timer for five minutes and I write. Stream of consciousness. No editing. Whatever is there.

If I think of something in the night I write it down immediately instead of trying to remember it. Trying to hold something in your mind to remember later is one of the fastest ways to stay awake. Put it in the notebook and let it go.

What changed for me

The looping stopped. Not immediately, but within a week or two I noticed I was getting into bed and my mind was actually quieter. Not silent. But quieter. The same thoughts weren't running through six times. They had somewhere to go.

Five minutes. A cheap notebook. I know it sounds too simple. That's what I thought too. Try it for a week before you write it off.

What finally got my kids to sleep through the night so I could too

I tried every gentle method, every schedule, every tip. Here is the honest version of what actually worked and what was just noise.

5 min readKids sleep

Nobody talks about this part. You can do everything right for your own sleep and still be completely wrecked because the kids are not sleeping. And if your partner is somehow sleeping through all of it, that is its own special kind of unfair. I was managing two sleep problems at the same time without realizing it. Mine and theirs.

Here is what I found after trying what felt like everything.

The routine has to be the same every single night

I thought I had a routine. Bath, book, bed. What I didn't realize is that a real bedtime routine is not just the activities. It is the exact same order, at the exact same time, starting earlier than feels necessary. Kids' nervous systems are wired for repetition in a way ours just are not. The sameness is the actual signal that it's time to wind down. When every night looks exactly the same, their body starts winding down before you even begin.

The change that surprised me most was starting earlier. I was starting too late. When kids are already overtired they produce cortisol to push through it, which is the opposite of what you need before bed. Starting the routine before they seem tired, not after, changed bedtime from a battle to something almost easy.

Starting the routine before they seem tired, not after. That one shift changed bedtime from a battle into something almost easy.

White noise in their room

I already had a white noise machine for myself and I had no idea it would be this effective for the kids too. Children sleep lighter than adults and wake up at sounds we don't even notice. The house settling. The TV from downstairs. A door closing. White noise layers over all of it and keeps them in a deeper sleep longer.

I put one in each of their rooms and the night wakings dropped noticeably within the first week. This is also the thing I recommend first to any parent who asks me about sleep because it is immediate, it is cheap, and it does not require changing anything else.

Screens off earlier than you think

Not an hour before bed. Two hours before bed for younger kids. The blue light issue is real but the bigger issue is stimulation. Their nervous systems are still processing everything they watched when they get into bed, which is why they lie there asking for water and telling you about things that happened at school six months ago. Moving screens back gave their brains time to actually slow down before we started the routine.

The thing that made bedtime feel safe for them

In my house, lying with the kids until they fall asleep is not an option and honestly it works perfectly for us. What I found instead is that children settle faster when they feel genuinely secure before you leave the room. That looks different in every household. For us it was a consistent closing ritual, the same words, the same order, every single night. Something small and predictable that signals the day is over and they are safe. Once that clicked, the "one more thing" requests got shorter and shorter and now bedtime is something they accept instead of resist.

The weighted blanket for kids, I was not expecting this one

I already had a weighted blanket for myself and at some point I wondered if it would do the same thing for the kids. I had heard other moms talking about it and I figured it was worth trying.

The idea behind it is something called deep pressure stimulation. The gentle weight on the body signals the nervous system to settle down. It raises serotonin, lowers cortisol, and tells the body it is safe and contained. For a child whose mind is still running when their head hits the pillow, that physical signal does something that words and routines alone sometimes cannot.

For my kids it worked. They fell asleep faster. The coming out of their room to find me stopped. I do not know if it is the blanket specifically or just the whole routine coming together but I know it is part of what changed things and I still use it.

It is not magic. But for a kid who has a hard time settling, the weight does something that words cannot always do on their own.

One thing worth knowing. Weighted blankets are not for babies or very young toddlers. The research and the safety guidelines are clear on that. For older kids, look for a blanket that is roughly 10 percent of their body weight. Nothing too heavy. And always check that they can move it off themselves easily.

The same studies that looked at kids with ADHD and anxiety found that children described feeling calm and safe under a weighted blanket in a way they couldn't with other sleep aids. Moms in parenting groups say the same thing. For kids who tend toward anxious energy at bedtime it is one of the most genuinely useful things I have come across.

When your kids finally sleep through, you will be stunned by how much better you feel. I had forgotten what it was like to wake up and not immediately feel behind.

We were in survival mode before. We didn't even know it.

The room temperature is everything

Getting the room to the right temperature changed the whole dynamic of our nights. Not too hot, not too cold, just that comfortable sweet spot where neither of us is fighting the covers or kicking them off. What I did not expect is that a comfortable room brought back something we had stopped doing without realizing it. We cuddle before sleep now. And sometimes that cuddle leads somewhere, which is good for the marriage and honestly good for sleep too. Physical intimacy releases oxytocin, which lowers cortisol, and your body naturally winds down after. He is asleep in about four minutes. I follow not long after. What actually fixed the snoring

What still helps on the hard nights

Some nights he comes to bed later than me. Some nights I am already deep asleep and the light or the movement pulls me out of it. The white noise machine handles almost all of that. The gut-sleep connection nobody explained to me properly

I kept treating my sleep and my stomach as two separate problems. They were never separate.

6 min readGut healthAffiliate links disclosed

For a long time my evenings had this pattern: bloated and uncomfortable after dinner, then wide awake at 1am or 3am for no obvious reason. I treated those as two unrelated annoyances. One was a digestion thing, one was a sleep thing, and I was trying to fix them separately, with separate articles, separate supplements, separate everything. It took embarrassingly long to realize they were the same problem wearing two different costumes.

What finally connected the dots for me was reading about the gut-brain axis, which is the constant two-way communication between your digestive system and your central nervous system. It runs largely through the vagus nerve, the same nerve I'd already been working on for the nervous system reset I wrote about here. Once I understood that, the bloating and the 3am wake-ups stopped feeling like separate issues.

Most of your serotonin is made in your gut, not your brain

This was the fact that changed everything for me. The vast majority of the body's serotonin, the figure usually cited is around ninety percent, is produced in the lining of your gut, not your brain. Serotonin is also the precursor to melatonin, the hormone that actually regulates your sleep-wake cycle. So if your gut lining is inflamed, or your microbiome is out of balance, that pipeline gets disrupted before melatonin even enters the picture. I had spent months trying to fix my melatonin production while ignoring the organ that makes most of the raw material for it.

Eating too late was sabotaging both things at once

I used to eat dinner around 8 or 8:30, sometimes later, and then wonder why I felt both stuffed and unable to switch off an hour later. Digestion and sleep are competing processes. When your body is still working hard on a heavy meal, it is harder for it to fully shift into the rest-and-digest state that good sleep depends on. Late alcohol made this worse in a way I hadn't appreciated either. It was not just affecting my sleep architecture directly, less deep sleep, less REM, it was also disrupting my gut bacteria overnight, which seemed to compound the next day.

Your gut does not know the difference between 8pm and 1am. But your sleep absolutely does, and it was paying for the gap.

The changes that actually moved the needle

I did not overhaul my entire diet. I made a handful of specific changes and gave them a few weeks. I moved dinner earlier, aiming for at least a three hour gap before bed instead of the usual one hour. I added more fiber and a couple of fermented foods most days, plain yogurt, kimchi, that kind of thing, instead of treating them as occasional extras. And I cut back on late evening alcohol specifically, even when I kept the same total amount, just shifting it earlier in the evening made a noticeable difference.

The small addition that helped most

Tart cherry juice became part of my evening routine alongside the earlier dinner. It is one of the few natural food sources of melatonin, and it is also anti-inflammatory, which lines up with the gut piece of this whole picture. I have a small glass with dinner rather than right before bed.

I tried the sleepy girl mocktail every night for two weeks. Honest thoughts.

Tart cherry juice, magnesium powder, sparkling water. Everyone said it worked. I had to find out for myself.

5 min readWellnessAffiliate links disclosed

I had been hearing about this mocktail for months before I actually made it. My hesitation was the pure tart cherry juice. The first time I made it straight, juice plus magnesium plus sparkling water, it was fine. Not something I was going to look forward to every night.

So I paid attention to what actually worked and tweaked it until it did. Two things changed everything. Raw honey to balance the tartness, and lime juice instead of lemon. Lime works with tart cherry. Lemon fights it. The elderberry syrup is optional but it rounds the whole drink out and I add it most nights.

Why it works

Okay bear with me because this is actually interesting and I promise it does not require a biology degree.

Your brain has a tiny gland called the pineal gland and its whole job is to produce melatonin when it gets dark outside. Think of it like a very reliable employee who only shows up when the lights go off. Tart cherry juice is one of the only foods that already contains melatonin, so when you drink it before bed you are basically sending your pineal gland a head start. Like showing up to work with coffee already made.

The tryptophan in the cherry juice is the same stuff everyone blames for giving you "The Itis" after Thanksgiving dinner. Your body converts it into serotonin first, which makes you feel calm and good, and then serotonin converts into melatonin. So you are getting melatonin two ways from one glass. Straight from the juice and made fresh by the power of God inside you. Overachiever beverage.

Then there is the magnesium. Your nervous system uses magnesium to activate the part of your brain that says chill... you are doing way too much. Without enough of it your nervous system stays in mild panic mode all night, which is why you fall asleep fine and then snap awake at 3am for no good reason. The glycinate form specifically gets absorbed well and crosses into the brain instead of just sitting in your gut. It also tells your adrenal glands to ease up on the cortisol, which is the stress hormone responsible for that wide awake the house is on fire feeling at 2am when nothing is even wrong.

Mix it real good together and 45 minutes before bed you'll have melatonin coming in from left and right. Your nervous system starts to get the signal to actually calm down, and your cortisol gets put on time out quick. Don't just take my word for it. Try it consistently for two weeks. You'll be making it in your head as soon as the kids and husband are fed. Your brain will start associating the drink with sleep before you even take a sip.

Fun fact: your body converts tryptophan into serotonin first, then serotonin into melatonin. So one sip is doing two jobs before it even gets to the good part.

The recipe

Recipe

The Inward Glow Sleepy Girl Mocktail

The version I actually make. Built to balance the tartness of pure tart cherry juice so it actually tastes good.

Ingredients

  • 2 oz pure tart cherry juice
  • 1 tsp magnesium glycinate powder
  • 1 tsp raw honey
  • 6 oz sparkling water
  • 1/2 tsp elderberry syrup (optional)
  • 1 tsp fresh lime juice (optional)
  • Ice

Method

  1. Add cherry juice and magnesium powder. Stir 30 seconds until fully dissolved.
  2. Stir in honey, elderberry syrup, and lime juice.
  3. Fill with ice. Pour sparkling water slowly over a spoon to keep the bubbles.
  4. Gentle stir. Drink 45 to 60 minutes before bed.

The honey is not optional if you are using pure tart cherry concentrate. Start with 1 tsp, taste, add more if needed. Lime over lemon every time. It works with the cherry instead of against it.

Everywhere I looked, people kept saying "regulate your nervous system" like it was a button you could press. Nobody explained what that actually meant or how to do it. So I went looking, and it turns out it is simpler than the wellness industry makes it sound.

The off switch you already have

Your nervous system has two main modes. One is the alert mode, the one that gets you through a deadline or a near-miss in traffic. The other is the rest mode, the one where digestion works properly, your heart rate slows down, and your body actually believes it is safe enough to sleep. The problem for a lot of us is that the alert mode never fully turns off. It just simmers in the background all evening, even when nothing is actually wrong.

The switch between the two runs through something called the vagus nerve. You cannot think your way into calm, but you can use your body to send the signal. That was the part nobody told me.

The thing that actually worked first

The first tool I tried was a breathing pattern: two inhales through the nose, one short and stacked right on top of the other, followed by one long, slow exhale through the mouth. It is sometimes called a physiological sigh. The first time I did it, I felt my shoulders drop about an inch without trying. That was the moment I realized this wasn't going to be another thing where I just had to "think positive" harder.

My nervous system was never getting the memo that the day was over. It just kept waiting for the next thing.

The second was almost embarrassingly simple: splashing cold water on my face and wrists before bed. There is a reflex, the kind that activates when your face hits cold water, that slows your heart rate down on its own. I do it while I'm already brushing my teeth, so it costs me nothing extra.

Building an actual wind-down

I used to think a wind-down routine meant "no screens after 9pm" and that was it. It wasn't enough on its own. What helped was giving my evenings an actual shape: dim lamps instead of overhead lights starting about ninety minutes before bed, and replacing scrolling with something low effort, journaling for five minutes, or just humming along to something quiet. Humming sounds silly but it vibrates the vagus nerve directly. I am not exaggerating when I say it works better than it has any right to.

He let me try his one night when I couldn't settle down, mostly to prove a point, and I went to bed annoyed and woke up wondering why I'd waited so long to ask for my own.

What deep pressure actually does

The mechanism is called deep pressure stimulation, and it is the same principle behind swaddling a newborn. Even, gentle pressure across the body tells your nervous system that you are safe, which lowers cortisol and increases serotonin, the precursor to melatonin. It is essentially a weighted, full-body hug that doesn't move or need anything from you.

Getting the weight right matters more than people say

The general guideline is about ten percent of your body weight, give or take a pound. Too light and it just feels like a regular blanket. Too heavy and it feels restrictive instead of calming, especially if you move around a lot in your sleep. I went slightly under ten percent the first time and it was the right call. You can always size up later.

Heavier is not automatically better. The point is even pressure, not maximum weight. A blanket that is too heavy just feels like something you need to escape from.

The two week test

The first couple of nights felt strange, more like adjusting to a new pillow than anything dramatic. By night four or five, I noticed I was falling asleep faster and staying asleep longer without realizing it was happening. The real test came when I traveled without it for a work trip. I slept noticeably worse, and it took me two nights home to fully connect the dots. That was when I stopped being skeptical.
